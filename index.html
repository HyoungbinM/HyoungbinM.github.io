About

I am a Ph.D. student in the Social & Cultural Psychology Lab at Yonsei University, advised by Young-Hoon Kim. My research addresses a central question in relationship science: why some romantic relationships flourish while others deteriorate. Rather than asking which individual characteristics predict relationship satisfaction, I ask with whom, when, and where they matter.

To date, my work has centered on three questions. First, how do partners’ characteristics interact in their associations with relationship satisfaction? I have examined partner effects and dyadic fit, asking whether similarity, complementarity, or the couple’s combined level is associated with better relationship outcomes, for characteristics ranging from attachment to saving orientation. Second, how does the importance of those characteristics vary across the broader environment? Partners are embedded in a wider context, and the same characteristic can matter more in some circumstances than in others. So far I have examined two such contexts, the transition to marriage and culture, comparing South Korea and the United States, and I am interested in extending this to other contexts, such as support from family and friends, economic strain, and major life stressors. Third, how do partners perceive each other? I have examined accuracy and bias in partner perception by comparing self-reports with partner reports of mate value and of personality, and I am particularly interested in the trade-off between accuracy and bias: under what conditions is accurate perception beneficial for relationship satisfaction, and under what conditions is bias?

My work draws on a wide range of data, including dyadic longitudinal studies of couples across the transition to marriage, large-scale surveys of married adults in South Korea and the United States, and national panel data from Korea and abroad. Methodologically, I specialize in dyadic and longitudinal analysis, including the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model, dyadic response surface analysis, and multilevel modeling, and I have recently begun to incorporate machine-learning approaches to forecast relationship outcomes.

Publications

Peer-Reviewed

  1. Moon, H., & Kim, Y. H. (2026). Prevalence and correlates of sexual inactivity among married couples. The Journal of Sexual Medicine, 23(7), Article qdag180. doi:10.1093/jsxmed/qdag180
    Summary

    Sexual inactivity among married couples is rising worldwide, yet cross-national comparisons have been rare. Drawing on four surveys of more than 5,000 married adults in South Korea and the United States, this paper shows that sexual inactivity is roughly twice as common in Korea at every age. Older age and a weaker promotion focus predicted inactivity in both countries, whereas less couple communication time, lower household income, and emotion suppression mattered mainly in Korea. The findings point to both universal and culturally specific pathways to sexual disengagement in marriage.

Under Review

  1. Moon, H., Cheon, J. E., Kim, D., Chae, M., Seo, M., Cohen, D., & Kim, Y. H. (under review). Compatibility is not about matching: The benefits of strong saving orientations in both partners. Psychological Science.
    Summary

    About one in three couples report fighting over money, but do partners need matching financial habits? Following Korean couples from six months before marriage to one year after, this study used dyadic response surface analysis to compare similarity, complementarity, and additive accounts of partners’ saving orientations. Satisfaction after marriage was highest when both partners were strong savers, a pattern that held after accounting for conscientiousness, income, and cohabitation. Financial compatibility appears to depend on the couple’s combined prudence rather than on how well partners match.

  2. Oh, C., Moon, H., & Kim, Y. H. (revise and resubmit). A partner who views themselves as more valuable than you, or one you view as more valuable than yourself? Two perspectives on mate value discrepancy and relationship satisfaction. Journal of Marriage and Family.
    Summary

    Is a “high-value” partner one who thinks highly of themselves, or one you think highly of? Prior work has treated partner mate value as a single construct, which may explain why findings on mate value discrepancy have been inconsistent. Following engaged couples across the transition to marriage with dyadic response surface analysis, this study separates the partner’s self-perceived mate value from the actor’s perception of the partner. The two perspectives pointed in opposite directions: satisfaction was higher when people saw their partner as more valuable than themselves, but lower when partners saw themselves as more valuable than the other. Only the actor’s view of the partner predicted gains in satisfaction after marriage.

  1. Kim, E.*, Moon, H.*, Kang, I., & Kim, Y. H. (in preparation). Who intends to have a child in the world’s lowest-fertility country? Predicting fertility intentions among premarital couples using machine learning.
    Summary

    South Korea’s fertility rate is the lowest in the OECD, yet little is known about which characteristics best distinguish premarital couples who want children from those who do not. Combining psychological, relational, economic, and demographic predictors from a cohort of engaged couples, this project uses cross-validated machine learning to rank predictors of fertility desire and intention when they are considered together rather than one at a time.

  2. Moon, H., & Kim, Y. H. (in preparation). Is personality more important than money and looks? The cross-sectional and longitudinal impact of pre-marital partner values (personality, appearance, and socioeconomic status) on relationship satisfaction.
    Summary

    Evolutionary and social accounts disagree about which partner qualities matter most. Following engaged couples before and one year after marriage, this study used the Actor–Partner Interdependence Model to compare how a partner’s personality, appearance, and socioeconomic status predict relationship and sexual satisfaction. Before marriage, personality mattered most for relationship satisfaction and appearance most for sexual satisfaction. One year into marriage, personality still predicted relationship satisfaction, appearance no longer did, and socioeconomic status began to matter. The pattern did not differ between men and women.

  3. Moon, H., Han, D. E., Oh, C., Chae, M., & Kim, Y. H. (in preparation). What predicts a satisfying marriage? Forecasting pre- and post-marital relationship satisfaction from premarital variables using random forest.
    Summary

    Using a cohort of more than 600 engaged couples, this project asks how well relationship satisfaction before and after marriage can be forecast from premarital characteristics, and whether a person’s own traits, the partner’s traits, differences within the couple, or sociodemographic factors carry the most predictive weight. Cross-validated random forests are compared with conventional dyadic models so that the conclusions do not depend on any single method.
